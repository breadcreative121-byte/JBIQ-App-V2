00

Purpose & authority

This chapter governs how JBIQ discloses results in voice. It defines the speak-the-shape rule, the four-beat voice pattern, the density ceilings, anchor selection, the voice + UI handoff contract, pure-voice fallbacks, degraded-mode behaviour, and the voice anti-patterns. With GA, it also governs cinematic voice experiences — multi-minute, persona-driven audio surfaces such as Grand Kundali Reveal and AI Anchor — and TTS prosody per content type.

It exists to:

  • Prevent voice from copying screen — voice is serial, screen is parallel.
  • Codify the four-beat voice pattern that carries the rhythm of Ch. 3 into spoken output.
  • Define density ceilings that respect auditory working memory.
  • Govern the voice + UI handoff so cross-modal desync is zero in production.
  • Add the cinematic pattern parallel to the four-beat, for multi-minute audio experiences.
  • Make TTS prosody decisions per content type, not per voice engine default.
Authority

This chapter is normative. A voice disclosure that violates the four-beat, the density ceilings, the anchor selection priority, or the cinematic rules must be flagged in review.

0.1 Relationship to other chapters

This chapter is the voice-specific case of the response anatomy (Ch. 4). The reply rhythm (Ch. 3) provides the temporal four beats that voice disclosure expresses in spoken form. Language and code-switching rules (Ch. 6) apply to voice equally — the four-beat is language-independent. The voice + UI contract is finalised here and referenced by multi-modal handoff (Ch. 10).

02

Voice invariants

  • Voice must not enumerate more than one option unprompted.
  • Voice must anchor on the user's stated priority, not on the result's ranking position.
  • Voice disclosure must end with a pivot. Ending on data is prohibited.
  • Where a visual surface is present and the result set is greater than one item, voice must name the visual handoff explicitly.
  • Voice must not recap the user's intent before answering.
  • Voice must not exceed the density ceilings defined in §5.
  • Voice disclosure at PARTIAL_RESULT_SHOWN must not use commitment language ("confirm?", "proceed?", "shall I go ahead?"). Commitment is a separate state.
  • Voice anchor must match the visually-rendered primary result. Mismatch is a cross-modal desync (Ch. 10).
  • Under degraded mode, voice must verbalise uncertainty before the anchor.
  • Cinematic voice experiences must hold a single persona across the whole arc — no mid-experience persona shifts.
03

Core principle

Speak the shape, not the list

Screen output can render five options in parallel; the user scans and picks. Voice output is serial. Reading the same five options aloud forces the user to hold them in working memory and compare mentally — this is the dominant cause of voice abandonment. Voice discloses the shape of the result, not the list.

Voice carries:

  • A count + outcome signal.
  • One anchor — the result that best matches the user's stated or inferred priority, disclosed in full.
  • The shape of the rest — collapsed into a range, pattern, or count.
  • A pivot — a handoff to the next action.

The full result set may only be read aloud serially when the user explicitly requests it, and even then only up to the ceilings in §5.

04

The four-beat voice pattern

All voice result disclosure follows the same four-beat structure. The beats appear in this order, always.

01
Outcome

"Found 3 morning flights."

Signal that results exist and how many.

02
Anchor

"Cheapest is 6:15 AM at ₹2,850."

Disclose one result in full, aligned to the user's priority.

03
Shape

"The others are a bit later, around the same price."

Collapse the rest into a range, pattern, or count. Never enumerate.

04
Pivot

"Want to book this one, or hear the others?"

Hand off to the next action. Never end on data.

Combined example

"Found 3 morning flights. Cheapest is 6:15 AM at ₹2,850. The others are a bit later, around the same price. Want to book this one, or hear the others?"

30 words. ≈ 10 seconds. Outcome → Anchor → Shape → Pivot.

06

Anchor selection

The anchor is the single result disclosed in full. Selection follows this priority order:

  • If the user stated an explicit priority ("cheapest", "earliest", "nearest", "highest rated"), anchor on that dimension.
  • If priority is inferable from memory at medium confidence or higher (the user always picks the ₹299 plan, always flies IndiGo), anchor on the memory-inferred priority.
  • If no priority signal exists, anchor on the canonical default for the domain — earliest for travel, nearest for location, lowest price for generic commerce, next-due for bills.

Never anchor on ranking position alone. "Option 1" is UI grammar and prohibited in voice. When anchor selection is ambiguous and the consequence is high, the system must clarify before disclosing rather than disclose and hope.

07

Screen offload — the voice + UI contract

When a visual surface is available, voice anchors and the screen carries detail. This is the voice + UI contract; it is the single most important rule when both surfaces are present.

7.1 When a visual surface is present and result set > 1

  • Voice discloses outcome, anchor, and shape.
  • Voice explicitly names the visual handoff: "I've put them on screen."
  • Voice offers both paths in the pivot: "Pick one, or ask me to read them out."
  • Voice anchor must match the visually rendered primary result. Mismatch is a cross-modal desync (Ch. 10 §10.4).

7.2 When no visual surface is present (speaker, IVR)

  • Voice discloses outcome and anchor only.
  • Pivot offers pull for next item or refinement: "Want to hear the next one, or change something?"
  • After the third item on explicit request, voice pivots to refinement rather than continue reading: "Want me to narrow these down?"

7.3 When the visual is offered but not yet seen

Smart speakers with companion screens, JioPhone with screen-off mode, or in-car displays — situations where the visual handoff is available but JBIQ cannot assume the user is looking. Voice anchors and names the handoff ("I've put them on screen if you're looking"); the pivot offers either path.

08

Pure-voice surfaces

Speaker, IVR, and feature phones without screen are voice-only by hardware. JBIQ does not adapt by guessing — the surface capability flag at the orchestration layer determines voice-only mode. The behaviour above in §7.2 applies. Two specific patterns matter:

8.1 Refinement in voice-only

Refinement is spoken, not tapped. The pivot offers the highest-impact filter. After one refinement, JBIQ re-anchors and offers either commit or another refinement — never enumerates the refined set unprompted.

Pattern

User: "Flights to Mumbai."

JBIQ: "Found 12 flights. Cheapest is 6:15 AM at ₹2,850. Want morning only, direct only, or all?"

User: "Direct only."

JBIQ: "Down to 5 direct flights. Cheapest stays at ₹2,850. Book this, or change anything?"

8.2 Commitment in voice-only

Commitment confirmation in voice-only goes through a separate auth channel — UPI deep-link via SMS, verbal confirm with voiceprint where available, or hand-off to the JBIQ app for the final commit. The voice four-beat governs disclosure; Ch. 8 governs the commitment itself.

09

Language & code-switching in voice

The four-beat pattern is language-independent. Anchor-first, shape-next, pivot-last applies across all 12+ production languages (GA Op. Goal 06) and in mixed-language (Hinglish, Tanglish, Banglish, etc.) disclosure. Disclosure language matches the language of the user's most recent intent utterance. A mid-flow code-switch by the user must not trigger a language reset for subsequent disclosure.

9.1 Examples

Grocery restock — Hinglish. Intent: "Ghar ka saman khatam ho gaya."

Voice — Hinglish

"BigBasket pe ₹847 ka cart ready hai. Atta aur dal hain. Screen pe dikhaya hai — confirm karein ya badlaav chahiye?"

Flight search — Hinglish. Intent: "Kal subah Mumbai ka flight dhoondo sasta wala."

Voice — Hinglish

"3 flights mile. Sabse sasta 6:15 AM ko, ₹2,850. Baaki thoda baad mein, same price ke around. Yeh book karein, ya aur options sunein?"

10

Degraded-mode behaviour

When the Memory Model signals degraded confidence, or result retrieval is partial, voice disclosure adapts:

  • Voice must verbalise uncertainty before the anchor — "I'm not fully confident, but…" or "Based on what I can see right now…".
  • Voice must not suppress the count when results are incomplete — "I found at least 3 — might be more when we're back online."
  • Voice must not apply Level 2 or higher autonomy defaults at PARTIAL_RESULT_SHOWN under degraded mode. The pivot must escalate to explicit confirmation.
  • Voice must not pre-commit to the anchor ("I've gone ahead and…") under degraded mode, regardless of autonomy level.

12

Cinematic voice experiences

GA addition

The four-beat governs transactional voice disclosure. GA introduces a parallel pattern for multi-minute, persona-driven audio surfaces — Grand Kundali Reveal, AI Anchor Daily Brief, AI Jockey, Cricket Match Companion. These surfaces are not transactional intents; they are narrative experiences. The rules below apply to them.

12.1 What "cinematic" means

A cinematic voice experience has four properties that distinguish it from a transactional disclosure: (1) duration measured in minutes, not seconds; (2) a defined persona that holds across the whole arc; (3) pacing that includes pauses, rhythm shifts, and emotional beats; (4) content that is narratively shaped — beginning, middle, payoff — rather than informationally shaped. Grand Kundali Reveal is the archetype; it is described in GA as a "cinematic voice-first walkthrough".

12.2 Pacing — slower than transactional

  • Words per minute: 130–150 (vs 160–180 for transactional disclosure).
  • Pauses: minimum 600ms at beat boundaries; up to 1.5s at emotional beats.
  • No back-to-back density. Each insight gets room to land before the next.
  • Length: 3–8 minutes typical. Longer with user opt-in.

12.3 Persona consistency

Every cinematic experience has a single defined persona — voice, register, vocabulary, prosody profile. The persona does not shift mid-experience. If the experience spans languages, the persona's character traits hold even as the language changes; only the linguistic surface translates.

Persona definitions are owned by the Voice & Multimodal pod and the vertical PM. The playbook governs how the persona is rendered, not who the persona is.

12.4 Attention modelling

A 5-minute audio experience must assume the user's attention will drift. JBIQ models this with three mechanisms: (1) recap moments at 60–90s intervals — a single sentence that brings the wandering listener back without insulting the focused one; (2) micro-pivots at natural breakpoints — "Shall I keep going, or pause here?" — every 90–120s; (3) checkpoint memory — the experience remembers where the user paused if they leave and come back.

12.5 Interrupt, invite, go quiet

Cinematic experiences live alongside other voice interactions. Three rules govern when they yield:

  • Interrupt — User input (any utterance) immediately pauses the experience. JBIQ acknowledges in one line ("Pausing — what's up?") and switches to the user's intent. Resume is offered after the new intent completes.
  • Invite — At natural breakpoints, JBIQ may invite related action ("Want to share this reading with someone?"). Invitations never force the user out of the experience.
  • Go quiet — Sensitive moments (Ch. 12), incoming calls, or explicit user request immediately silences the experience. Resume is opt-in only.

12.6 Worked example — Grand Kundali Reveal (opening 90s)

Voice — Hindi

[Opening — 600ms pause after user opens the experience]
"Aapka Janma Kundali tayar hai."

[800ms pause]
"Saath baith jaaiye — kuch khoobsurat baatein bataani hain. Pehle aapke Lagna se shuru karenge, kyunki yahi aapka asli swabhav dikhata hai."

[1.2s pause — emotional landing]
"Aapka Lagna hai Vrishabh — Taurus. Iska matlab hai…"

Persona: warm, unhurried, knowledgeable. Pace ~135 wpm. Pauses are deliberate. Content unfolds like a story.

13

TTS prosody per content type

GA addition

Different content classes need different prosody. A devotional reading is paced and reverent. A news brief is crisp and informational. A coaching session is encouraging and pause-rich. A transactional confirmation is clipped and confident. The TTS engine is configured per content type — not one voice for everything.

Content typePaceRegisterPause patternExamples
Devotional120–140 wpmWarm, reverent, unhurriedLong pauses at insight boundaries (1.0–1.5s)Live Darshan, Sacred Calendar, Talk to Your Planets
News / anchor150–170 wpmCrisp, informationalShort pauses between segments (300–500ms)AI Anchor Daily Brief, Fact Checker verdict
Coaching130–150 wpmEncouraging, patientPauses after questions (800–1200ms)Microlearning, English Learning, Interview Prep
Transactional160–180 wpmConfident, clippedMinimal pauses; one at pivot (300–400ms)Recharge confirm, bill pay, plan switch
Cinematic130–150 wpmPersona-defined, variesVariable; up to 1.5s at emotional beatsGrand Kundali Reveal
Sport / live170–190 wpmEnergetic, present-tenseAlmost none; pace varies with eventCricket Match Companion live commentary

13.1 Acceptance criteria for prosody

  • Each production language meets TTS MOS 4.0+ per content type.
  • Cross-content prosody profiles are A/B tested and signed off before any cinematic ships.
  • Prosody profile mismatches (e.g., a coaching session rendered with transactional pace) are flagged in the regression suite as a structural defect.
